ELECTRICAL CIRCUIT ANALYSIS

Norton’s Theorem

Reducing any linear network to one current source and one resistor

Tahsin Khanom & Nisha Barua Tusty
Introduction to Electrical Engineering (EEE 124)

THE THEOREM

A linear and bidirectional two-terminal network can be replaced by an equivalent circuit consisting of a Current source IN in parallel with a resistor RN

Norton current IN

The current through a short circuit placed across the two terminals.

Norton resistance RN

The resistance seen from the terminals with all independent sources turned off.

Equivalent

IN in parallel with RN behaves identically at the terminals for any load.

Turning sources off: voltage sources become shorts, current sources become open circuits.

STEP BY STEP

The four steps

1 · Remove the load

Take out RL, the component of concern, and label terminals a and b.

2 · Find RN

Short all voltage sources and open all current sources, then find R between a and b.

3 · Find IN

Short a to b and use any circuit-analysis method to get the short-circuit current.

4 · Find IL

Draw IN ∥ RN, reconnect RL, then IL = IN·RN / (RN + RL).

With a dependent source, never turn it off. Find RN as Voc / Isc instead.

ANALYSIS METHODS

Six ways to find the Norton current

Method Best when What it gives
Series–parallel + Ohm’s law Shorted circuit is simple IN = V / R total
Nodal analysis (KCL) Many nodes, current sources Node voltages, then IN
Mesh analysis (KVL) Many loops, voltage sources Mesh current in the short
Superposition Several independent sources Add each source’s IN
Source transformation Source–resistor pairs to merge One current source
Thévenin conversion VTh already known IN = VTh / RTh

With dependent sources, use nodal or mesh analysis and keep the control variable, or use IN = Voc / RN.

SAME CIRCUIT · FOUR METHODS

All give IN = 1.5 A

12 V source, R1 = 2 Ω, node X with R3 = 4 Ω to ground, R2 = 4 Ω to terminal a. Short a–b.

Nodal

KCL at X: (12 − V) / 2 = V / 4 + V / 4, so V = 6 V. IN = 6 / 4 = 1.5 A.

Mesh

12 = 2I1 + 4(I1 − I2), 0 = 4I2 + 4(I2 − I1). I1 = 3 A, so IN = I2 = 1.5 A.

Source transformation

12 V with 2 Ω becomes 6 A ∥ 2 Ω. The 6 A splits by conductance (0.5, 0.25, 0.25 S): the short takes 1.5 A.

Thévenin conversion

VTh = 8 V, RTh = 16/3 Ω. IN = 8 / (16/3) = 1.5 A.

EXAMPLE 1 · INDEPENDENT SOURCE

24 V, 6 Ω in series, 12 Ω shunt, 4 Ω load

1 · Original

Load RL = 4 Ω across a–b.

2 · Find IN

Short a–b. The 12 Ω is bypassed, so IN = 24 / 6 = 4 A.

3 · Find RN

Short the source. RN = 6 ∥ 12 = 4 Ω.

4 · Load current

IL = 4 × 4 / (4 + 4) = 2 A.

EXAMPLE 2 · DEPENDENT SOURCE

12 V, 4 Ω, 2 Ω, dependent source 2·Ix, load 1.2 Ω

Ix is the current in the 4 Ω. The dependent source stays in the circuit.

IN = 9 A

Short a–b: the 2 Ω carries nothing. Ix = 12 / 4 = 3 A, the source gives 6 A, so IN = 3 + 6.

Voc = 7.2 V

KCL at a: Ix + 2Ix = Va / 2 with Ix = (12 − Va) / 4.

RN = 0.8 Ω

Voc / IN = 7.2 / 9. Then IL = 9 × 0.8 / 2.0 = 3.6 A.

FACTS AND LIMITS

Why it matters, and where it stops

Key facts

Published 1926 by Edward L. Norton of Bell Labs. Hans F. Mayer reached it independently. Maximum power reaches the load when RL = RN.

Applications

Fast load-change analysis, transistor and amplifier models, current-driven sensor circuits.

Limitations

Linear circuits only. Diodes and other non-linear parts break it. The equivalence holds at the terminals, not inside.
